# Minnesota fraud oversight — statutory authority crosswalk

**As of:** 2026-08-29  
**Purpose:** Bind recurring claims about who could stop payments, investigate fraud, prosecute cases, or administratively disqualify providers to controlling Minnesota law.

## Medicaid payment controls

Minn. Stat. § 256B.064 places the administrative sanctions described there with the Commissioner of Human Services. Those sanctions include suspension or withholding of payments, monetary recovery, and provider suspension/termination. For a credible allegation of fraud with a pending investigation, the statute generally requires withholding or reducing payments unless a federally recognized good-cause exception applies.

This means the administrative power to stop or withhold Medicaid provider payments is distinct from the Attorney General's prosecutorial role. It does **not** answer whether the factual prerequisites for a particular provider were satisfied on a particular date.

## Medicaid investigation and referral

Minn. Stat. § 256B.04, subd. 10 requires the Medicaid agency to maintain methods for identifying and investigating suspected fraud and to inform the Attorney General in writing when the facts may warrant civil or criminal proceedings.

Minn. Stat. § 256B.12 separately authorizes the Attorney General or appropriate county attorney to institute specified criminal or civil Medicaid actions and recovery proceedings. This is direct subject-matter authority; it should not be generalized to every Minnesota criminal matter.

## General criminal prosecution authority

Minn. Stat. § 388.051 gives county attorneys the general duty to prosecute felonies. Minn. Stat. § 8.01 provides routes for Attorney General participation: a county attorney may request the AG's appearance, and the Governor may request in writing that the AG prosecute an indictable offense. Other statutes, including § 256B.12, can grant direct subject-specific authority.

Accordingly, statements such as “the Attorney General can prosecute only when a county attorney asks” are incomplete as a general proposition.

## BCA Financial Crimes and Fraud Section

Minn. Stat. § 299C.061 creates the BCA Financial Crimes and Fraud Section. Covered suspected state-program fraud of at least $100,000 is generally subject to mandatory state-agency referral to the Section, which then has discretion to investigate or route the matter elsewhere. The statute expressly preserves Attorney General authority and expressly carves out Medicaid referrals that must go to the state Medicaid Fraud Control Unit under federal/state Medicaid law.

The Section was created in 2025. Its referral rules must **not** be projected backward onto Feeding Our Future-era conduct as if they existed in 2019-2022.

## What changed for CCAP in 2019

2019 First Special Session, chapter 9, article 2, section 107 created an administrative-disqualification procedure for child-care providers accused of intentional program violations when criminal action had not been pursued. The law provided that intent in that administrative process could be established through a pattern of conduct violating program rules; on appeal, the issuing agency bore a preponderance-of-the-evidence burden.

That is **not** a lowering of the criminal burden of proof. It is an administrative remedy, with an administrative evidentiary standard, used when criminal prosecution had not been pursued. Section 107 did not contain a separate effective-date clause; Minnesota's default effective-date rule in § 645.02 applies absent another specified date.

## Publication consequences

1. Keep **payment-control authority** separate from **criminal/civil prosecution authority**.
2. Do not describe the 2019 CCAP change as “lowering the criminal fraud standard.”
3. Do not back-project the 2025 BCA fraud-referral law onto earlier scandals.
4. Any claim that an official *should have exercised* a statutory power requires the contemporaneous factual record showing the statutory predicates were actually met.
