# Minnesota Medicaid — findings, controls, and consequences through 29 August 2026

**Machine-readable timeline:** `research/oversight/medicaid-control-timeline.json`  
**14-service matrix:** `research/cases/medicaid-hcbs/high-risk-services-matrix.json`  
**Recovery ledger:** `research/money/recovery-ledger-2026-08-29.json`

This report separates four questions that are routinely collapsed:

1. What did federal/state oversight bodies find about Minnesota's controls?
2. What did Minnesota change in response?
3. What criminal cases exist in particular services?
4. What did those changes cost or do to legitimate service delivery?

## The federal finding is an administrative compliance finding, not a criminal case

CMS's January 2026 Federal Register notice says the Administrator found Minnesota's Medicaid agency **substantially out of compliance** with federal program-integrity requirements in Social Security Act §1902(a)(64) and 42 CFR Part 455, Subpart A. CMS cited weaknesses in proactive identification, analytics and monitoring, and said it had raised concerns with Minnesota previously.

CMS also described the 14 high-risk services as consuming approximately **$3.75 billion** in federal and state resources. That amount is program spending/resources, not a $3.75 billion fraud finding.

CMS described an estimated quarterly federal-funding withholding formula of **$515,154,947.56** subject to the federal hearing/compliance process. That figure is a funding-control formula, not a loss measurement.

Source: `research/sources/src-cms-fr-2026-00512-minnesota-program-integrity.json`.

## The same federal government separately measured a 2.16% PERM improper-payment rate

CMS's RY2025 PERM notice measured Minnesota's overall Medicaid improper-payment rate at **2.16%**, using a 900-review sample covering payments from July 1, 2023 through June 30, 2024.

That does not contradict the program-integrity finding because it is a different measurement:

- PERM asks whether sampled payments met payment/documentation/eligibility rules under a defined methodology.
- CMS's January compliance action asks whether Minnesota had sufficiently effective systems to identify, investigate, prevent and refer fraud/waste/abuse, especially in high-risk services.
- CMS itself says an improper payment can be technically improper even when made to the right recipient for the right amount and no recovery is required.
- A PERM improper-payment rate is **not a fraud rate**.

Source: `research/sources/src-cms-2026-01-20-mn-perm-ry2025.json`.

This distinction is essential for the public site because both numbers have been used rhetorically in ways their source documents do not support.

## HSS was a long-running federal-state control issue before the 2026 compliance notice

CMS says it began working with Minnesota on HSS fraud concerns through UPIC audits in **July 2024**, gave Minnesota preliminary findings from three HSS provider audits in **April 2025**, and transferred those audits to the state for investigation at Minnesota's request in **June 2025**.

Minnesota's revised CAP says DHS requested CMS assistance on **August 1, 2025** to terminate HSS. The benefit closed after October 31, 2025.

This chronology should be shown alongside the HSS criminal cases rather than presenting the cases as if no government control activity preceded the charges.

## Minnesota's revised response was concrete and measurable

Minnesota's January 30 revised CAP committed to, among other measures:

- off-cycle revalidation of the high-risk provider universe;
- unannounced/in-person site visits and ownership/background/credential checks;
- an April 1 provider-response deadline and May 31 revalidation target;
- enhanced prepayment review using Optum analytics;
- routing unresolved flagged claims/provider behavior to policy/program teams and potentially the DHS Office of Inspector General;
- enrollment moratoria and other provider-screening controls.

DHS says revalidation requirements went to **5,640 providers** in the plan-stage universe. These were not 5,640 fraud suspects; they were providers subject to heightened screening.

Source: `research/sources/src-mn-dhs-2026-01-30-revised-cap.json`.

## Optum produced large numbers, but their definitions matter

Optum's Phase 1 report analyzed 46 months of **fee-for-service** paid claims; managed care was outside scope. It reports:

- **>$52.3M** in what the report calls “direct recoveries” — defined as funds that can be recouped based on identified issues/policy violations;
- **>$1.7B** in potential recovery opportunities — including vulnerabilities that may require policy clarification or medical-necessity review;
- **>$165M** in cost avoidance;
- **>$21M** in paid-claim provider findings sent to the state for review;
- **>$2.9M** in prepay analytic findings.

The first three figures are not interchangeable. In particular, the report does not establish that the entire $52.3M had already been collected in cash, and the $1.7B is emphatically not proven fraud.

Source: `research/sources/src-mn-optum-2026-01-30-phase1-payment-integrity.json`.

## CMS then deferred $259.5M in federal matching funds

On February 25, CMS announced a **$259,505,491 deferral** associated with Minnesota's Q4 FY2025 Medicaid spending while review continued. CMS described approximately $243.8M as unsupported or potentially fraudulent claims and $15.4M as related to immigration-status documentation.

A deferral is not a final disallowance and not a criminal loss finding. The final disposition of those deferred funds remains an important research gap.

Source: `research/sources/src-cms-2026-02-25-minnesota-medicaid-deferral.json`.

## CAP approval is presently asymmetric in our primary-source record

Minnesota DHS states CMS approved the revised CAP on **March 19, 2026**. We retain that as a state agency statement.

We have not yet captured the standalone March 19 CMS approval letter on an official CMS host. Until then, the proper formulation is:

> Minnesota says CMS approved the revised CAP on March 19.

not:

> We independently hold CMS's March 19 approval letter.

That difference is small rhetorically but important evidentially.

## Revalidation outcomes are administrative outcomes

DHS's August 12 snapshot reports:

- **3,070 providers revalidated**;
- **1,627 appealing disenrollment**;
- **128 chose termination rather than start revalidation**;
- **660 had appeal windows close and are terminated**.

Those outcomes tell us about provider-screening/compliance processes. A provider appealing or being administratively terminated is not thereby a criminal defendant.

DHS separately reports 761 inactive provider agencies terminated in October 2025 and another 18,109 inactive providers terminated through March 2026 as part of CAP cleanup, saying none had billed Medicaid in the preceding year.

Source: `research/sources/src-mn-dhs-2026-08-program-integrity-snapshot.json`.

## Controls can create real service-access costs, and those deserve tracking too

The eventual public site should not measure anti-fraud policy only by how many providers it removes.

### EIDBI

On August 25, DHS said its medical-review agent was experiencing **longer-than-required service-authorization timelines** because of increased review volume and was increasing capacity/prioritizing high-risk renewals and initial cases.

That does not establish that anti-fraud review caused every delay, but it is a legitimate operational consequence to track.

### NEMT

On August 24, DHS announced Myers & Stauffer claims-compliance reviews for NEMT providers through 2026. Selection for review is not an accusation.

### Recuperative Care

All recuperative-care services require prior authorization as of August 1.

### CFSS/PCA

DHS says extended PCA services end September 30 as the transition to CFSS continues.

These belong in a **reform effectiveness** layer: what was changed, what happened to suspicious/improper claims, and what happened to eligible people's access to care.

## What we can say today

The evidence supports all of the following simultaneously:

- Minnesota Medicaid has multiple proven/pleaded and charged fraud cases in particular services.
- CMS made a serious administrative finding about Minnesota's program-integrity systems.
- Minnesota implemented substantial controls in response and had also begun some high-risk program work before January 2026.
- PERM's 2.16% improper-payment measurement is real but is not a fraud rate and does not negate the separate system-control concerns.
- Large recovery/deferral figures cannot be combined into one “fraud” or “money recovered” number.
- Aggressive controls can affect legitimate providers and beneficiaries, so access/timeliness metrics belong beside enforcement metrics.

That is a much more useful public story than either “Minnesota did nothing” or “the low error rate proves the fraud concerns were false.” Neither simplification survives the source record.
