# PRECEDENT — Minnesota public-program fraud corpus

**Compiled:** 26 August 2026. Holdings taken from official opinions (supremecourt.gov PDFs; 8th Circuit slip opinion as fetched).  
Wikipedia and law-firm blogs were used only as search leads.

**Proposition map:** These cases define the *legal elements* of private fraud (A) and civil FCA liability. They do not themselves prove Minnesota facts. They do constrain what prosecutors must prove when charging Minnesota Medicaid / nutrition / grant fraud.

---

## 1. Universal Health Services, Inc. v. United States ex rel. Escobar, 579 U.S. 176 (2016)

- **Citation:** 579 U.S. 176 (2016); No. 15-7
- **Official opinion PDF:** https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/15pdf/15-7_a074.pdf
- **Bound volume / LII locator (official pagination):** 579 U.S. 176
- **Date:** Decided 16 June 2016
- **Evidence class:** `JUDICIAL_HOLDING`
- **Holding (2–4 sentences):** The False Claims Act’s “false or fraudulent” language incorporates common-law fraud, including **implied false certification**: a defendant can be liable for omitting information about noncompliance with statutory, regulatory, or contractual requirements that makes the submitted claims misleading. Not every undisclosed violation is actionable. The misrepresentation must be **material** to the Government’s payment decision — a “demanding” standard looking to the effect on the likely or actual behavior of the recipient, not merely to whether the Government labeled a requirement a condition of payment. The Government’s payment of claims despite actual knowledge of violations is strong evidence the requirement was not material.
- **Relevance to MN program fraud:** Controls civil FCA (31 U.S.C. § 3729) and Minnesota 15C cases built on “you billed Medicaid while out of compliance with enrollment, licensure, or waiver rules.” Materiality is the brake: a paperwork breach the State routinely paid anyway is a weak FCA theory. *Kousisis* later cites *Escobar*’s demanding materiality standard as the limit on fraudulent-inducement wire fraud as well.

## 2. United States ex rel. Schutte v. SuperValu Inc., 598 U.S. 739 (2023)

- **Citation:** 598 U.S. 739 (2023); No. 21-1326 (with No. 22-111, *Proctor v. Safeway*)
- **Official opinion PDF:** https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/22pdf/21-1326_6jfl.pdf
- **U.S. Reports version:** https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/22pdf/598us2r30_g2bh.pdf
- **Date:** Argued 18 April 2023; decided 1 June 2023. Unanimous; Thomas, J.
- **Evidence class:** `JUDICIAL_HOLDING`
- **Holding:** FCA scienter turns on the defendant’s **subjective knowledge at the time it submitted the claim** — actual knowledge, deliberate ignorance, or reckless disregard, 31 U.S.C. § 3729(b)(1). A defendant who believed its claims were false cannot defeat scienter by later pointing to an objectively reasonable interpretation of an ambiguous regulation (*Safeco* does not control). Facial legal ambiguity, standing alone, does not preclude a finding that the defendant “knew.”
- **Relevance:** Minnesota providers who internally treated discounted, kickback, or non-rendered services as nonbillable, then billed Medicaid at full authorized rates, cannot hide behind post hoc “the rule was ambiguous.” Equally, honest disagreement about a genuinely unsettled billing rule, *without* contemporaneous subjective falsity, is not FCA knowledge. Distinguishes **A** (knowing false claims) from **C** (negligent documentation, which Minn. Stat. § 15C.02(d) also excludes).

## 3. United States ex rel. Cairns v. D.S. Medical LLC, 42 F.4th 828 (8th Cir. 2022)

- **Citation:** 42 F.4th 828 (8th Cir. 2022); Nos. 20-2445, 20-2448, 20-3009, 20-3010
- **Official 8th Circuit slip opinion:** https://ecf.ca8.uscourts.gov/opndir/22/07/202445P.pdf  
  (curl from this environment returned 404 on 26 Aug 2026 after a successful WebFetch of the same URL; secondary official-text PDF: https://cases.justia.com/federal/appellate-courts/ca8/20-2445/20-2445-2022-07-26.pdf)
- **Date:** Filed 26 July 2022. Stras, J., joined by Loken and Arnold, JJ.
- **Evidence class:** `JUDICIAL_HOLDING` (binding in the Eighth Circuit, which includes Minnesota)
- **Holding:** When FCA falsity is premised on the 2010 AKS amendment, 42 U.S.C. § 1320a-7b(g) — a claim that “includes items or services **resulting from**” an AKS violation — “resulting from” imposes **but-for causation**. The government must prove the defendant would not have included particular items or services in the claim *absent* the kickbacks. A jury instruction that falsity is shown merely because the claim “failed to disclose” the AKS violation is error. The AKS violation, as an element of a civil FCA case, is proved by a **preponderance of the evidence**, not beyond a reasonable doubt (§ 3731(d) — “all essential elements”).
- **Relevance:** Minnesota EIDBI parent-kickback cases and any FCA theory that “the claim was tainted.” In the 8th Circuit, taint is not enough under (g); the kickback must have been a but-for cause of the billed items. Other falsity theories (express false statements that services were rendered) do not need this causal showing. Parallel state hook: Minn. Stat. § 609.542, subd. 6, uses the same “resulting from” phrase for 15C.02.

## 4. Ciminelli v. United States, 598 U.S. 306 (2023)

- **Citation:** 598 U.S. 306 (2023); No. 21-1170
- **Official opinion PDF:** https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/22pdf/21-1170_b97d.pdf (local: `pdfs/ciminelli-21-1170.pdf`)
- **Date:** Argued 28 November 2022; decided 11 May 2023. Thomas, J.
- **Evidence class:** `JUDICIAL_HOLDING`
- **Holding:** The Second Circuit’s “right-to-control” theory is not a valid basis for federal wire-fraud liability. Section 1343 criminalizes only schemes to deprive the victim of **traditional money or property interests**, not “potentially valuable economic information” needed to make discretionary economic decisions. Information as such is not the protected property interest. (The Court declined to reach an alternative fraudulent-inducement theory on that record.)
- **Relevance:** Federal prosecutors in Minnesota cannot treat “DHS was deprived of accurate billing information” as the property obtained. They must identify money or property as the object of the scheme (Medicaid dollars, nutrition reimbursements). *Kousisis* later holds fraudulent inducement is **not** a relabeling of right-to-control, because it protects money/property, not information.

## 5. Sabri v. United States, 541 U.S. 600 (2004)

- **Citation:** 541 U.S. 600 (2004); No. 03-44
- **Official opinion PDF:** https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/03pdf/03-44.pdf
- **Date:** Decided 17 May 2004
- **Evidence class:** `JUDICIAL_HOLDING`
- **Holding:** 18 U.S.C. § 666(a)(2) (bribery of agents of entities receiving federal funds) is a valid exercise of Congress’s spending / necessary-and-proper power. Congress may criminalize bribery of state and local officials of entities that receive federal program funds **without** proving a direct connection between the bribe and a particular federal dollar. The $10,000 federal-benefits threshold supplies the jurisdictional hook.
- **Relevance:** Confirms § 666 can be used against bribery/theft involving Minnesota agencies, counties, tribes, and nonprofit sponsors (MDE, DHS, CACFP sponsors, CCAP providers) that receive >$10,000 in federal benefits, even if the bribe was not traced to a labeled federal draw.

## 6. Fischer v. United States, 529 U.S. 667 (2000)

- **Citation:** 529 U.S. 667 (2000); No. 99-116
- **Official opinion PDF:** https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/99pdf/99-116.pdf
- **Date:** Decided 15 May 2000
- **Evidence class:** `JUDICIAL_HOLDING`
- **Holding:** Medicare payments to a hospital qualify as “benefits” under 18 U.S.C. § 666. “Benefits” is not limited to noncommercial subsidies; it includes federal assistance provided for the promotion of the general welfare through health-care financing. The statute’s $10,000 jurisdictional threshold is satisfied by such program payments.
- **Relevance:** Places Medicaid/Medicare provider payments, and by analogical reasoning other federal assistance streams (CACFP, CCDF/CCAP, behavioral-health block grants), inside § 666’s “benefits” concept when charging theft or bribery by agents of recipient organizations.
- **Do not confuse with:** *Fischer v. United States*, 603 U.S. 480 (2024) (No. 23-5572), which construed a different statute, 18 U.S.C. § 1512(c)(2) (obstruction). The 2000 case is the one specified for this corpus.

## 7. Kousisis v. United States, 605 U.S. ___ (2025)

- **Citation:** *Kousisis v. United States*, 605 U.S. ___ (2025) (slip opinion); No. 23-909. Affirming 82 F.4th 230 (3d Cir. 2023).
- **Official slip opinion:** https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/24pdf/23-909_f2q3.pdf (local: `pdfs/kousisis-23-909.pdf`)
- **U.S. Reports page-proof PDF:** https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/24pdf/605us1r32_f2ah.pdf
- **Dates:** Argued 9 December 2024; decided **22 May 2025**.
- **Opinion:** Barrett, J., delivered the opinion of the Court, joined by Roberts, C.J., and Thomas, Alito, Kagan, Kavanaugh, and Jackson, JJ. Thomas, J., filed a concurrence (materiality skepticism on these facts). Gorsuch, J., concurred in part and in the judgment (would preserve a traditional benefit-of-the-bargain injury rule). Sotomayor, J., concurred in the judgment (would stop at rejecting a net-economic-loss element).
- **Evidence class:** `JUDICIAL_HOLDING`

### Precise holding (from the syllabus and opinion of the Court)

> **Held:** A defendant who induces a victim to enter into a transaction under materially false pretenses may be convicted of federal fraud even if the defendant did not seek to cause the victim economic loss.

Restated in the Court’s own words: the fraudulent-inducement theory “supports liability for federal fraud anytime a defendant uses falsehoods to induce a victim to enter into a transaction.” A defendant “violates § 1343 by scheming to ‘obtain’ the victim’s ‘money or property,’ regardless of whether he seeks to leave the victim economically worse off.” “The wire fraud statute is agnostic about economic loss.”

**What still must be proved (limits):**

1. **Deception plus money-or-property as an object** of the scheme (*Ciminelli* / *Kelly* line). Regulatory licenses and intangible unconnected interests remain outside § 1343.
2. **Materiality of the falsehood** is an element (*Neder*; *Escobar*’s “demanding” materiality cited as narrowing the theory). The Court **did not** decide the precise materiality test for § 1343 (traditional inducement test vs. “essence of the bargain”), because petitioners did not contest materiality.
3. Fraudulent inducement is **not** the *Ciminelli* right-to-control theory: it protects money and property, not “mere information.”
4. Providing a contractual quid pro quo of equal market value is **not** a defense if the victim was tricked into the transaction by a material lie (Judge Hand / *Rowe* quotation adopted).

**Facts in brief:** Kousisis/Alpha won PennDOT painting contracts by falsely representing DBE subcontracting; Markias was a pass-through. The painting work was satisfactory and Alpha made >$20 million gross profit. Convictions under §§ 1343 and 1349 affirmed.

### Relevance to Minnesota program fraud

- Lets prosecutors charge **A** where a provider induced DHS/MDE enrollment or a grant award by material lies (ownership, qualifications, disadvantaged-business status, capacity to serve) **even if some services were delivered and the State “got a meal / an hour of care.”**
- Does **not** abolish materiality. Thomas, J., concurring, doubted DBE promises went to the essence of a bridge-painting bargain; analogously, a Minnesota defendant will argue that a lied-about demographic, training certificate, or DBE-style condition was not material if the State paid identical rates for the same hours. That fight is now the main event.
- Does **not** convert **B/C** (agency failure to monitor) into a crime by the agency. The statute still requires a private scheme to obtain money or property by material false pretenses.
- Gorsuch’s concurrence flags the babysitter / employee-fib hypothetical: lower courts remain divided on how much “injury” beyond parting with money is required. Cite the majority, note the concurrence.

## Cross-walk: what each case is *for* in a Minnesota file

| Case | Use when the file is about… | Not a proof of… |
|---|---|---|
| *Escobar* | Civil FCA implied-certification / materiality of enrollment or waiver conditions | That a MN provider committed fraud |
| *SuperValu* | FCA scienter; subjective belief vs. post-hoc ambiguity | Criminal mens rea (different statutes) |
| *Cairns* | AKS → FCA “resulting from” but-for cause (8th Cir.) | That a kickback occurred |
| *Ciminelli* | Wire fraud must target traditional property, not “right to control information” | Honesty of MN officials |
| *Sabri* | Constitutionality of § 666 bribery without tracing to a federal dollar | A bribe was paid |
| *Fischer* (2000) | Medicaid/Medicare payments are § 666 “benefits” | Amount of loss |
| *Kousisis* | Fraudulent inducement; no net-loss requirement; materiality still required | $9 billion, or that DHS “lost” value equal to billed amounts |
