// 01 — Why this exists

Truth is not the opposite of peace.

Peace without truth becomes denial. Truth without care becomes a weapon. Minnesota needs neither.

The purpose of MN Peace is to make the public record easier to see, then hold the line between what the documents actually say and what anger, fear, politics, or repetition may tempt us to infer.

Look directly

Name what happened at the level the evidence supports.

Open the filing. Identify the alleged conduct. Track the case. Ask where controls failed. Do not make precision the enemy of accountability.

Refuse contagion

Do not let a case number spread guilt beyond the people and conduct it names.

A neighborhood, faith, language, ethnicity, surname, or shared address is not a defendant. Evidence does not become stronger when blame becomes broader.

The work here is both: look harder, blame more carefully.

// 02 — What the record shows

Follow the cases, not a giant number.

The published record currently follows two families of federal cases. They overlap in public conversation, but they are not one case, one theory, or one pool of money.

The point is not to flatten them into a single scandal. It is to let each filing carry its own defendants, dates, allegations, procedural status, and dollars.

// 03 — How to read it

Four distinctions change the whole story.

Most bad summaries collapse categories the court record keeps separate. We do the opposite.

  • Charged is not convicted. An indictment or information records an allegation by prosecutors.
  • Billed is not paid. Paid is not alleged loss. Alleged loss is not proven loss.
  • The same dollars can appear more than once. Restatements and nested amounts should not be added together.
  • Status changes over time. A plea, verdict, dismissal, or judgment should change what the site says when the source supports it.

// 04 — Accountability

Accountability belongs where the evidence puts it.

A careful public record can hold more than one truth at once: allegations against named defendants, legitimate questions about oversight and administration, harm to taxpayers and program recipients, and no basis for collective guilt.

01

People answer for their own conduct.

Charges, pleas, verdicts, dismissals, and judgments belong to the people and entities the court record actually names.

02

Institutions can be examined without inventing crimes.

Oversight, controls, incentives, staffing, warnings, and administrative choices are fair subjects for evidence-based scrutiny.

03

Communities are not defendants.

No language, faith, neighborhood, ethnicity, immigrant group, or diaspora inherits the allegations made against an individual.

Peace is not a request to look away. It is a refusal to look lazily.

// 05 — What this site owes you

The record should become clearer as it grows.

These are publication promises, not internal research rules. They are the standard the public site should be judged against.

01

Primary sources lead

Whenever we can publish or link the underlying filing, you should be able to reach it from the summary.

02

Procedural status is visible

A charge, plea, conviction, dismissal, and sentence are different events. The language should change when the record changes.

03

Dollars keep their meaning

Billed, paid, received, alleged loss, program spending, and proven loss are not interchangeable.

04

Corrections beat defensiveness

If a source or interpretation changes, the public page should change with it and the correction should be traceable.

05

No manufactured statewide total

Overlapping cases and repeated dollar figures will not be stacked into a number the underlying record cannot support.

06

No communal shortcut

Ethnicity, religion, language, neighborhood, or immigration background will not be used as a substitute for evidence.

// 06 — Repair

A public scandal can damage money and trust.

The first can be counted. The second is repaired by how we investigate, govern, talk to one another, and remember what happened.

Repair starts with a disciplined sequence: know what is alleged, know what is proven, ask what systems failed, protect the people public programs were meant to serve, refuse collective blame, and update your conclusions when the record changes.

Minnesota is more than the worst document in its public file. That is not a reason to hide the document. It is a reason to read it well.
